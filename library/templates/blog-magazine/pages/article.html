Arkansas was once the apple capital of America. Four orchards are trying to bring it back.

By Nell Carver, editor of The Ozark Ledger · October 18, 2026 · 14 min read

In 1900, two counties in northwest Arkansas shipped more apples by rail than any place in the United States. By 1950 almost every tree was gone. This fall, in four orchards within an hour of each other, the old varieties are fruiting again.

The boom nobody remembers

The numbers are hard to believe now. In 1901, Benton County alone had more than two million apple trees. Rail cars left Rogers and Springdale by the hundred every autumn, bound for Kansas City and St. Louis, and the Ben Davis apple, tough, bland and nearly indestructible in transit, made fortunes. The Arkansas Black, a better apple by every measure except patience, was the premium crop.

Then three things happened in a decade. The codling moth arrived and growers had no spray that worked. A freeze in April 1917 killed blossom across the whole region. And a county agent named Ernest Riggs started talking to farmers about chickens.

Four orchards, one idea

Mara Whitlock planted her first Arkansas Blacks in 2014 on a ridge her grandfather had cleared for cattle. She now has 1,100 trees across eleven heirloom varieties, and a cider house that sells out by December. Twenty miles south, the Danforth brothers grafted scion wood from a single surviving 1890s tree they found behind a church in Winslow. Two more growers, in Elkins and near Pea Ridge, are working from the same handful of old trees.

Every grower in this valley a hundred years ago knew something we had to relearn from scratch. We are not pioneering anything. We are picking up a dropped thread.

Mara Whitlock, Hollow Ridge Orchard

What the old varieties taste like

We tasted nine of them on a cold morning in Mara's packing shed. The Arkansas Black straight off the tree is firm to the point of hostility and tastes mostly of tannin; after eight weeks in storage it turns dense, winey and faintly of cinnamon. The Shannon, a yellow apple nobody has grown commercially since the 1930s, is all honey and almost no acid. The Ben Davis is exactly as dull as its reputation.

  • Arkansas Black: the keeper. Buy in October, eat at Christmas.
  • Shannon: sweet, soft, gone in a week. Pie apple.
  • Collins Red: the one the growers argue about. Sharp, aromatic, inconsistent.
  • Ben Davis: historically important, culinarily pointless.

The orchards are not trying to recreate 1900. Nobody wants two million trees. The idea is a few thousand, sold at the farm gate and pressed into cider, with a story that is true. On the evidence of this October, it is working.
